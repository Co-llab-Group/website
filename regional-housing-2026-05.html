§ 01 · The Diagnosis

Subsidy alone can't fix this.

The room agreed on more than it disagreed on. Elected officials, agency staff, financiers, builders, and intermediaries arrived at the same diagnosis.

Cincinnati's housing system isn't failing because of money. It's failing because the funding sources don't coordinate. Because there's no equity capital to do the rebuilding work. And because community trust is treated as a per-project task instead of as shared infrastructure.

Subsidy is being used to patch problems it wasn't designed to fix. Broken permitting. Mismatched funder rules. Markets too damaged to attract private equity. Community engagement done deal by deal.

§ 02 · Sector One

Government.

City Council and Economic Development · County · Planning Commission · Ohio General Assembly · HUD · Federal Reserve Bank
What is happening

The public sector is fielding three pressures at once. A growing supply gap. Shrinking fiscal tools as ARPA dollars run out in 2026. And contradictory pressure from the community.

In one evening, a city staffer was told that a single neighborhood has too much affordable housing, too much subsidized market-rate housing, and too much vacant land sitting still.

County leaders described tools that were built for a simpler problem. The gap used to be purely financial. Now it's also political, informational, and infrastructural. State legislators are caught between home-rule traditions and the need for state-level reform on zoning and permitting. Federal officials see local readiness, not federal capacity, as the bottleneck for the next wave of housing legislation.

Concerns
  1. Misperception is the political tax. The label "affordable housing" carries connotations that hurt projects, no matter what they actually deliver.
  2. Tools are misaligned with today's gaps. Subsidy stacks designed a decade ago don't close today's construction costs.
  3. The infrastructure tail is unfunded. Sewer lift stations, utility upgrades, and brownfield demolition eat into housing budgets.
  4. Home rule cuts both ways. State mandates create political blowback. State encouragement alone doesn't have enough teeth.
A good deal looks like
  1. Community engagement is done before the deal hits a planning commission agenda.
  2. Mixed-income at the neighborhood scale, not income-segregated.
  3. Bundled with infrastructure, workforce, or school-stabilization outcomes.
  4. Funding sources pre-coordinated. The public role is one decision, not five.
  5. Policy-ready, so the project can absorb new federal or state programs the moment they land.

§ 03 · Sector Two

Capital and Finance.

CDFIs · Land Banks · Federal Home Loan Banks · GSEs · Mission-aligned institutional capital
What is happening

Capital is being deployed, but in pieces too small and too short-dated to move the market. CDFIs described "piecemeal fundraising," meaning small pools recycled on short terms. That structurally limits how much concessionary lending can do.

Developer behavior is a function of equity behavior. Developers aren't the bad guys. They're the visible end of a chase for equity, and equity won't enter broken markets.

Federal banks operate inside fixed regulator constraints that prevent any single national underwriting standard. The GSEs report that Cincinnati's challenges aren't unique nationally. That's both reassuring and a reason to stop solving these problems in isolation.

Concerns
  1. Equity is the missing layer. Debt plus subsidy plus civic-minded equity capped near 3% returns doesn't attract real scale.
  2. Funder rule fragmentation is the silent tax. Davis-Bacon wages can shift up to closing day on FHA deals.
  3. The capital stack closes too early. Sewer and water reviews come after permits are pulled, after contingency is already spent.
  4. Subsidy is treated as gap-fill, not as a return on investment. It should be measured in units created.
  5. Legal review is a transfer tax. On $20 to $40 million deals, it's the single largest line item after construction.
A good deal looks like
  1. One coordinated intake. Single application, single underwriting conversation.
  2. A meaningful equity layer in the stack. Not 100% tax credits. Not booster-only.
  3. A long-dated, low-cost regional capital pool that lets concessionary lenders make longer commitments.
  4. A shared, transparent pipeline visible to CDFIs, land banks, mission capital, and tail-end donors.
  5. Mixed-income in markets that can plausibly attract national institutional equity.

§ 04 · Sector Three

Developers.

Public housing authorities · Single-family infill developers · Market-rate multifamily · Mixed-income builders · Small-scale affordable builders · Realtors
What is happening

Every developer described a variation of the same gauntlet. Market health, then zoning, then community engagement, then advocacy, then council and incentives. A project that survives all five within a predictable timeline is rare.

The Evanston case study showed what it takes to turn a broken market. About $5M of patient loss, over five years, before a private builder can take over and make money. The system has no vehicle to absorb that initial loss at scale.

Small-scale builders without tax credits reported four-year project cycles, funded $1M at a time. Market-rate developers reported losing investor interest to lower-friction markets in other states.

Concerns
  1. Equity at the front of the deal is the number one barrier. Civic equity caps near 3%. The pool is small.
  2. Scale is not designed in. Lots, permits, and engagement all happen one at a time.
  3. The permitting back end blows up the budget. Second-tier utility reviewers come in after permits are issued.
  4. Construction jobs are invisible in incentive scoring.
  5. Tax delinquency on absentee-landlord property goes unenforced.
  6. Community Benefits Agreements, as currently written, are deal-killers for outside private equity.
  7. Tax Increment Financing dollars are not consistently deployed for housing and infrastructure as the statute intends.
A good deal looks like
  1. Site-work, utilities, and permitting pre-cleared before lots come to market.
  2. Lots released in batches large enough for economies of scale (20 to 40 lots).
  3. Community engagement done at the neighborhood-plan level. Done once. Not relitigated per project.
  4. A single funding intake yielding a coordinated capital stack within a predictable timeline.
  5. Tax enforcement on chronic absentee-landlord property to level the field.
  6. Community Benefits Agreements structured to coexist with national equity expectations.
  7. Construction jobs and local subcontractor employment counted in incentive math.
§ 05 · The Through-Line

Five themes the room already agrees on.

Four shared diagnoses emerged across the three sectors. A fifth reframes housing's economic role. None of them will move the supply curve alone. Together, they describe a system reform.

i.

Coordination beats subsidy.

Fragmentation across funders, permitting, community engagement, and legal review costs more than any single financing gap. And it's more recoverable. A coordinated front door is the highest-leverage move available to the region.

ii.

Equity is the missing capital layer.

Debt and subsidy don't build mixed-income housing in markets that need rebuilding. The region needs a long-dated, patient, equity-like pool. Large enough to absorb the early losses that turn broken markets. Structured to work alongside subsidy and outside institutional equity.

iii.

Mixed-income at neighborhood scale is the unlock.

The current toolset funds concentrated affordable housing in already-disinvested neighborhoods. Every sector at the table called this suboptimal. The fix is the symmetric move. Moderate housing in disinvested neighborhoods. Affordable housing in market-rate neighborhoods.

iv.

Community engagement is infrastructure.

When community work is done per-project, every project pays the same tax. The projects that survive aren't the best projects. They're the ones with the highest tolerance for friction. A neighborhood-level engagement framework, funded as infrastructure, removes that tax permanently.

v.

Housing is economic, educational, and health infrastructure.

43% of Cincinnati renters are cost-burdened. One in four are severely cost-burdened. In 31 of 42 city public schools, 60 to 80% of students are off-track for kindergarten readiness. Chronic absenteeism is at 45%. These are housing outcomes showing up as workforce, school, and health outcomes. Any reform measured only in units built will undercount its own value.

§ 06 · What "At Scale" Looks Like

Eight components.
Each one names the complaint it answers.

The convening wasn't asked to choose among these. It was asked to name them. And to acknowledge that no single organization can deliver any one of them alone. Each component below maps directly to a complaint heard in the room.

  1. 01

    A regional coordinating intake.

    One front door for projects under a defined scale, pulling together CDFIs, land banks, city, county, and aligned philanthropic and mission capital.

    Funder fragmentation · The per-source cost and timeline penalty
  2. 02

    A long-dated regional housing capital pool.

    Patient, equity-like capital. Large enough to absorb early-stage losses in turnaround markets, and to take an equity position alongside national institutional capital in mixed-income deals.

    The equity-layer gap · Evanston-scale subsidy requirement
  3. 03

    Pre-cleared neighborhood plans.

    Engagement done at the plan level, including pattern-book systems for ADUs and small multi-units. Individual projects inherit zoning and engagement instead of re-litigating them.

    The gauntlet · Per-project community tax
  4. 04

    Unified permitting and second-tier utility review.

    One review window that includes sewer, water, and building. Before permits are issued, not after.

    Contingency blow-up at construction
  5. 05

    Tax-delinquency enforcement track.

    Targeted at chronic absentee landlords, paired with land-bank inventory transparency so local investors can participate.

    Playing field · Local investor access
  6. 06

    A credit-recognition standard.

    Rental-payment history recognized as a credit input, advanced jointly through realtors, GSEs, and state-level legislation. To expand the qualified-buyer pool.

    Qualified-buyer ceiling · Renter-to-owner transition
  7. 07

    An updated incentive framework.

    Counts construction jobs, local subcontractor spend, and supply-side outcomes. Structures Community Benefits Agreements to coexist with institutional equity.

    Regional competitiveness for outside equity
  8. 08

    Cross-sector outcome metrics.

    Units created, school-mobility reduction, workforce stability, healthcare cost. Owned jointly by housing, schools, workforce, and health systems.

    Housing as economic infrastructure
The Forward Question

The room is willing.
The diagnosis is shared.
Which component goes first?

The natural next step is simple. For each of the eight components, identify the smallest viable coalition that could move it from diagnosis to commitment in the next 12 to 18 months. Then name a convening cadence that holds the work between now and then. The Greater Cincinnati Foundation is positioned to be that holding pattern.
